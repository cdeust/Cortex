# ADR-1109: Recall pools keep their whole boundary tie group

## Status

accepted. Amends ADR-1106 rule 1 for the five `recall_memories()` pools and the SQLite recall signals; ADR-1106's tie-breaks everywhere else (the final cut, listings, hooks, entity lookups) are unchanged.

## Context

`recall_memories()` (`pg_schema.py`) builds five candidate pools, each cut at `v_pool = p_max_results * 10` rows: `vec` (cosine distance), `fts` (`ts_rank_cd`), `ngram` (trigram similarity), `hot` (`effective_heat`), `recency` (`created_at`). ADR-1106 made each cut deterministic with `created_at DESC, id DESC`. When more rows than the pool share the boundary score, that tie-break still chooses which of them the signal credits, and the score supports none of the choices:

- LoCoMo conv-41 (122 rows, `v_pool` 100): every row sits at `effective_heat` 0.05. Before ADR-1106 heap order dropped sessions 26-32 from `hot`; with ADR-1106 sessions 1-6 drop. Each choice loses the questions whose evidence sits in the dropped sessions: newest-first lost conv-41 questions 4, 6 and 9, which all five main runs answered, and LoCoMo moved to R@10 0.8870, MRR 0.7780, under main's five-run range (0.8875-0.8900, 0.7795-0.7811).
- The `fts` cut was inside a tie group on 192 of conv-41's 193 calls, `recency` on 193 of 193.
- On the owner's store (69,143 memories, read-only, 50 live queries, 2026-10-15): the pool was full and cut inside a tie group for `fts` on 45 of 50 queries (boundary groups up to 349 rows past the cut), `ngram` 20, `hot` 20, `vec` 3, `recency` 0.

On PostgreSQL the fusion is score-based: a pool row contributes `w * raw / max(raw)`, so rows inside a pool that tie already contribute equally; the defect is only membership. On SQLite the fusion is rank-based, `w / (k + rank)` (Cormack, Clarke and Buettcher, SIGIR 2009): tied rows got distinct ranks from the tie-break, so even the rows the cut kept were credited by age.

## Options measured

1. **Keep the whole boundary group (`FETCH FIRST n ROWS WITH TIES`).** A row is in a pool iff fewer than `v_pool` rows score strictly higher: the union of the pools every tie-break would give. Cost grows with the boundary group.
2. **Drop the whole boundary group** (cut at the last complete score level): the intersection of the pools every tie-break would give. Bounded cost, but it discards what the signal does know: the boundary group outranks every row below it. When the top score level alone holds more than `v_pool` rows the signal vanishes entirely: conv-41's `hot` pool would be empty, and on a store where more than `v_pool` rows sit at `no_decay` heat 1.0, heat would stop contributing at all.
3. **Rank equalisation for ties (dense or shared rank).** Not applicable to PostgreSQL's score-based fusion, where equal scores already give equal contributions; it is the SQLite half of the fix, below.
4. **Heat and recency as per-row features** (score every candidate, not only the pool's rows). It changes the fusion model, applies to two of five pools only, and leaves the `fts`, `ngram` and `vec` cuts, the ones that cut ties most often, as they are.

Option 1 is the only one that keeps every tie-break-independent fact the score states. Its cost was measured before choosing it (Benchmark).

## Decision

1. **PostgreSQL: every pool is `ORDER BY <score> FETCH FIRST (v_pool) ROWS WITH TIES`.** The ADR-1106 keys are removed from the five pool ORDER BYs: PostgreSQL defines a tie on the whole ORDER BY list, so with `id` in it no two rows would ever tie. The order inside a pool is irrelevant to the fusion (each pool feeds a `UNION` of ids and its own `raw_score`). On an exact (sorted) plan a row is in a pool iff fewer than `v_pool` rows score strictly higher. The `vec` pool may instead plan the HNSW index under `hnsw.iterative_scan = relaxed_order` (ADR-1105), whose rows arrive "slightly out of order by distance" (pgvector README): `WITH TIES` then finds the boundary group in arrival order, and the pool stays approximate as it already was on main. The final `ORDER BY (superseded_by_id IS NOT NULL), final_score DESC, created_at DESC, id DESC LIMIT p_max_results * 3` keeps ADR-1106's total order. `WITH TIES` needs PostgreSQL 13; pgvector 0.8.0 (ADR-1105) already sets the floor above it.
2. **SQLite: the same tie rule, and tied rows share their positions' credit.** `tied_rrf` gives each row of a tie group occupying positions a..a+g-1 the mean of `w / (k + r)` over those positions: the expected credit over every ordering of the group, the treatment of tied scores in McSherry and Najork, "Computing Information Retrieval Performance Measures Efficiently in the Presence of Tied Scores" (ECIR 2008). The group's total credit equals what any single tie-break would hand out, so a signal's weight is unchanged. Every row of a group gets the same credit, so a group that runs past the pool is never read row by row: the FTS5 (`bm25(memories_fts) = boundary`), heat (`heat_base = boundary`) and recency (`created_at = boundary`) signals return it as a `TieGroup`, a predicate on `memories m` with that one credit, after counting it (`group_credit` keeps `tied_rrf`'s exact summation; the count is the one O(group) step, an index-only scan). Rows reached only through such groups share the score of the exact set of groups they belong to (plus the agent boost, times the trust factor), so each class, at most 7 group sets times 2 agent and 2 trust cases, is read with one `ORDER BY created_at DESC, id DESC LIMIT max_results * 3` (`sqlite_store_recall_fill.py`); the merged candidates are ranked as before. Scores add up in signal order (vector, FTS5, heat, recency) from 0, the fusion's own arithmetic. Heat and recency groups are counted and filled through covering indexes ordered for it, `idx_memories_heat_tie` (`heat_base, capture_origin, agent_context, created_at, id`) and `idx_memories_created_tie` (`created_at, capture_origin, agent_context, id, heat_base`), each followed by the other columns the count and the fill test (`is_stale, superseded_by_id, is_global, domain, directory_context`), so neither reads a table row or sorts; they are named with `INDEXED BY` because without `ANALYZE` statistics SQLite picks a narrower index and sorts the whole group, and they are created after the column migrations, since they cover migrated columns. A group's size is counted once every signal has produced its group (`sqlite_store_recall_groups.py`). When the recall has more than one group, that one index-only pass is grouped by (capture_origin, agent_context), in index order, and per pair also totals the rows lying in each combination of the other groups, so each class's rows per pair follow by inclusion-exclusion. With a single group, the size is a plain count and the pairs are listed by a loose index scan, one seek per pair. The rows of a class are then read through its smallest indexed group: production always splits a class by trust (factor 0.7) and, with an agent topic, by agent; every (origin, agent) pair belongs to exactly one such partition, and each pair holding rows of the class is read with one equality seek on the covering index, newest first with no sort and `LIMIT max_results * 3`, all seeks of a partition in one statement (pairs bound as one JSON array). Classes and partitions without a row cost no seek. The seeks are bounded by the store's vocabulary, not by the group: five capture origins (`core/capture_origin.ALL_ORIGINS`) times the agent contexts present (owner's store: 419 pairs in all, at most 231 in one domain). Measured and rejected on the production arguments: one `LIMIT` scan per partition over a `(heat_base, created_at)` index walks the group for every sparse partition (12 to 155 ms at 250,000 rows on one heat, 9bd39a31 and 21e29796); one `ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY agent, trust ...)` statement per class sorts the whole class (42 to 74 ms against 4.6 to 14.9 ms at 40,000 rows). `group_credit` sums its terms with `map()` (bit-identical to the generator on 20,000 random cases, 8.8 to 4.3 ms over 250,000 positions), and one-row credits are computed inline so they do not evict a large group's credit from its cache. The vector signal: sqlite-vec's KNN refuses k above 4096 (`SQLITE_VEC_VEC0_K_MAX`, checked in `vec0Filter_knn`, sqlite-vec v0.1.9 `sqlite-vec.c`). The KNN reads `pool + 1` rows and, while the last still ties the boundary, doubles k up to 4096; a pool needing more (`max_results` ≥ 410, reachable through `unified_search`, whose `max_results` has no maximum) or a group still open at 4096 is read with the scalar `vec_distance_l2(embedding, ?)`, which computes the same `distance_l2_sqr_float` (on 5,000 random rows the KNN's and the scalar's distances were equal on every row). The KNN stays the default because the scalar scan is far slower: 8.5 ms against 345 ms at 40,000 vectors, LIMIT 101 (2026-10-16, arm64). The vector group is at most 4096 rows on the KNN path and is read as rows. A vector-signal failure raises; it no longer degrades silently to the other signals. Every set of ids reaches SQLite as one JSON array read through `json_each(?)`, so no statement depends on the build's bind-variable limit (`SQLITE_MAX_VARIABLE_NUMBER`). The code is split by responsibility: `sqlite_store_recall.py` (fusion, ranking), `sqlite_store_recall_fill.py` (classes of group rows), `sqlite_store_recall_signals.py` (vector, heat, recency), `sqlite_store_search.py` (FTS5 signal and search listings).
3. **Existing installs get the new function on the next store open** (schema hash, ADR-0545); `test_pg_recall_ddl_upgrade.py` installs the b2e63876 body and asserts the store replaces it.

The two backends share the tie rule, not the whole membership. On main already, and unchanged here: SQLite cuts the vector and FTS5 pools before the eligibility filters (heat, domain, directory and stale are applied when the results are read back), where PostgreSQL filters `eligible` before every cut; SQLite's heat signal ranks stored `heat_base` and keeps `post_tool_capture` rows, where PostgreSQL ranks `effective_heat` and excludes them; SQLite has no trigram pool.

Out of scope, so the omission is a decision: the final `LIMIT p_max_results * 3` (a ranking of fused scores, tie-broken by ADR-1106), and the `recency` pool's membership in `candidates` when `p_w_recency` is 0 (pre-existing: such rows are materialised but never reach `fused`; `WITH TIES` can add a whole same-timestamp session to that set).

**Owner decision, 2026-10-16: the exact group count stays.** Each tied row's credit is the mean over the positions its group occupies, so an exact score needs the group's size under the query's own filters (min_heat, domain or global, directory or global, not stale, current); SQLite has no constant-time range count, and the alternative, an aggregate table kept by triggers on every heat write, was declined. The count costs about 2 ms at 40,000 and 15 ms at 250,000 memories on one heat, and nothing measurable with a real heat spread.

Results are identical to 7be0f243, 9bd39a31 and 21e29796 (the last two filled by scans): same ids, same order, same score bits on the 16 tie-cut, deterministic-tie and limit scenarios (1,350 rows) and on the 63 latency cases above. `test_sqlite_recall_fill_property.py` checks the same against a naive reference that reads every signal in full with no LIMIT: 60 random stores with 2 to 3 values per signal, a directory and a domain filter, two agent values and skewed origin and agent mixes (no trusted row, only trusted rows), 300 queries, 237 of them with groups past the pool and 218 returned rows coming from the fill; it also checks every per-pair class count against a naive count (417 pairs, two or more groups in 54 of 60 stores). `scripts/recall_fill_mutants.py` applies 13 mutants to a copy of the code and runs the property and tie-cut tests on it; 12 are killed and one is equivalent (seeking pairs without class rows returns no row), recorded in `benchmarks/results/mutants/recall-fill-20261016.txt`. `test_sqlite_recall_tie_limits.py` pins the engine limits: a 3,000-row vector pool with a tied boundary pair and 3,300 identical embeddings keep every row's vector credit; a pool past 4096 (`max_results` 410, and `unified_search(max_results=69)`) returns results; no statement's placeholder count grows with a tie group (150 against 1,200 tied rows, equal; 150 against 1,200 on 7293672d). These tests pass on Python 3.10.18 and 3.13.7.

## Consequences

Positive: a pool's membership is a function of the scores alone. Tests: `tests_py/infrastructure/test_pg_recall_tie_cut.py` puts 130 rows on one tied score per pool and gives one of them, the oldest or the newest, a second signal; it must rank first, alone. On origin/main b2e63876 the five oldest cases fail; the newest cases pass there and pin that the fix is not a reversed tie-break. `test_sqlite_recall_tie_cut.py` does the same for the four SQLite signals and also checks the answer's exact credit (the group's mean plus rank 1 of the second signal); all eight fail on b2e63876, the newest cases because the newest row got rank 1's credit instead of the group's. `tests_py/shared/test_tie_groups.py` pins the three helpers. Negative: LoCoMo R@10 0.8860 and MRR 0.7768, under ADR-1106's 0.8870 / 0.7780 and main's range; the traced losses come from tie groups the old cut happened to split in the answer's favour, carried forward by recall's heat write-back (Benchmark). A pool can exceed `v_pool` by its boundary tie group, unbounded in principle: on the owner's store the largest group added 349 rows to `fts` and recall got faster at p50 and 54 ms slower at the paired p95. On SQLite a group past the pool costs its exact count, an index-only range count linear in the group (owner decision above): on a store where every memory shares one `heat_base`, 11.6 to 20.4 ms at 250,000 memories against about 3 ms (Benchmark). Existing SQLite stores build the two covering indexes once, on the first open after upgrade (a 250,000-memory store written by b2e63876: 0.32 s once, then 0.03 s per open; the file grows from 113.5 to 142.2 MB with these short synthetic rows, less in proportion with real content). Opening a store whose file is read-only fails on b2e63876 already, before any index is built: at `_configure`'s `PRAGMA journal_mode=WAL` when the directory is read-only too, otherwise in `_run_migrations` (the team-decision backfill `UPDATE`, line 162); this change adds no read-only failure, and read-only opens are not a supported path (no code opens the SQLite store read-only).
